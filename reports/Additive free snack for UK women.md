# Target midlife women first, then test the gym crowd

On the evidence available, Ewan and his co-founder should make **health-conscious women of roughly 35–55 (Segment B) their first early-adopter target**, with the sharpest focus on women who have digestive niggles or are in perimenopause. The reason is less about which group is "bigger" and more about fit. An additive-free bar can realistically deliver about **6–10g of protein** with soft texture and decent shelf life. That is a strong offer next to the 7–9g natural bars that midlife women are already being sold, but a weak one next to the 20g, maltitol-sweetened Barebells and Grenade bars that dominate the young gym-goer's shelf. Segment B also worries more about ultra-processed food, has documented high rates of bloating, has more money to spend, and is served in mainstream shops by only two clean, women-focused bars, both under 10g protein. Segment A (women aged 20–26 who go to the gym) is easier to reach from Durham and snacks more often, but it is crowded, price-sensitive and wants protein numbers a clean recipe struggles to hit. The case for B is moderately strong, not conclusive. No current UK data splits willingness to pay or bar buying by age and gender. So the last six weeks of the programme should be used to test both segments side by side with real samples and real money, against decision rules set in advance. The plan for that is at the end of this report.

## A £0.4–0.7bn bar market where only protein, fibre and "clean" are growing

The UK snack bar market is mature and is growing only through price rises. Mintel reports that value sales rose **4.6% in 2025 while volume fell 3.3%** ([Mintel](https://store.mintel.com/report/uk-snack-bars-and-breakfast-biscuits-market-report)). Size estimates depend on definitions: GlobalData puts UK cereal bars at **£434.65m in 2024** ([GlobalData](https://www.globaldata.com/store/report/uk-cereal-bars-market-analysis/)), while Mintel's wider "snack bars and breakfast biscuits" category was **over £700m in 2024, forecast to reach £845m by 2029** ([Mintel](https://store.mintel.com/report/uk-snack-bars-and-breakfast-biscuits-market-report-2025)). Either way, a new bar has to win share from existing brands. It cannot rely on the category growing underneath it.

The growth sits in a few pockets. Circana data in The Grocer's 2025 sports nutrition ranking shows **protein bar value up 16% and units up 5.2%**. Within that, the long-time leader Grenade lost **16.2% of value**, while Huel grew **60.9%** ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/rankings/sports-nutrition-2025-protein-bars-surge-despite-upf-debate/712930.article)). NIQ found that **cereal bars with a protein claim grew about 20%**, and across all groceries **fibre-based foods grew 14.1% and protein-based foods 9.6%**, against 4.1% for groceries overall, over the six months to late January 2026 ([NIQ](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2026/uk-shoppers-shift-to-healthier-diets-in-2026-fuelling-growth-in-protein-and-fibre-sales/)). Supermarkets are acting on fibre. Lidl set the UK's first fibre sales target ([Lidl](https://corporate.lidl.co.uk/media-centre/pressreleases/2025/lidl-is-first-uk-supermarket-to-introduce-fibre-boosting-sales-targets)), and Sainsbury's put "Full on Fibre" labels on **500+ products** in June 2026 ([Food Manufacture](https://www.foodmanufacture.co.uk/Article/2026/06/09/sainsburys-promises-to-up-fibre-in-uk-diets-with-new-products-and-labelling-that-flags-fibre/)).

Two background forces push in this brand's favour. The first is concern about ultra-processed food (UPF), which is high and steady: **78% of adults said they were concerned in June 2026**, second only to food prices ([FSA](https://science.food.gov.uk/article/165444-consumer-insights-tracker-april-2026-june-2026)). Commentators now argue that "protein bars solved taste; the next wave will solve trust" ([The Food Stack](https://thefoodstack.substack.com/p/protein-bars-solved-taste-the-next)). The second is weight-loss jabs (GLP-1 drugs such as Mounjaro). Worldpanel says **6.3% of British households** now include a user, up from 2.3% in 2024, and crisps and confectionery are the hardest-hit categories ([Grocery Gazette](https://www.grocerygazette.co.uk/2026/09/11/mounjaro-effect-deepens-as-weight-loss-drugs-reshape-uk-grocery-spending/)). Among users, **42% eat more protein and 34% more fibre** ([NIQ/IGD](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2026/80-of-uk-households-with-a-glp-1-user-say-it-affects-eating-habits-of-everyone-in-the-home/)). A small, gentle, protein-and-fibre snack fits that shift better than a big indulgent bar does.

There are warning signs too. Protein is turning from a selling point into a basic expectation, and a backlash is building. **57% of UK shoppers** say they follow a high-protein diet to some extent, but Google searches for "protein washing" rose **488%** in a year ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/analysis-and-features/protein-mania-are-shoppers-getting-too-much-of-a-good-thing/722201.article)). Gut-health interest is also narrowing towards specific symptoms. Tastewise finds most gut-health associations declining except a few such as anti-bloating ([Tastewise](https://tastewise.io/blog/uk-food-trends)). Stated concern also runs ahead of behaviour: protein bars kept growing through the 2025 UPF debate, and snack servings chosen for fibre rose only **1.1%** ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/category-reports/the-brands-capitalising-on-fibremaxxing/718536.article)). For Ewan, the lesson is that "clean" will not sell a bar on its own. It has to taste as good as what shoppers already buy, at a similar price.

## Segment B has the stronger need; Segment A has the easier reach

The table below sets the two groups side by side. Most of the age-split evidence is older, comes from surveys commissioned by brands, or is from outside the UK, so treat it as direction rather than precise measurement.

**Segment A's case is real and should not be dismissed.** These women snack more often, already "speak protein", are growing fast as a fitness audience (women's fitness participation rose 1.7% in 2024–25 against 0.7% for sport, per [ukactive evidence to Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/166594/html/)), and are drawn to Pilates and "strong, not bulky" wellness, which suits pastel branding. They are also a short walk from Ewan's front door. The difficulty is what they compare a bar against. For a gym-goer, protein grams are the headline and taste is what decides the purchase. Mintel says taste remains the key driver in UK snack bars despite UPF worries ([Mintel](https://store.mintel.com/report/uk-snack-bars-and-breakfast-biscuits-market-report-2025)). An additive-free bar with 8g of protein would sit next to Barebells at 20g, and Barebells is the fastest-growing brand largely because it tastes like a chocolate bar. Young shoppers also push back on price: Warrior's founder says shoppers don't want to spend £3 on a protein bar ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/rankings/sports-nutrition-2025-protein-bars-surge-despite-upf-debate/712930.article)).

**Segment B's needs line up with what the product can actually do.** "No additives, no sweeteners, gentle on the gut" is a buying reason for someone who reads labels, distrusts UPF and gets bloated. To a 22-year-old after a workout it is a nice extra. Women and older adults read labels more, though the best study is old and American ([Prepared Foods](https://preparedfoods.com/articles/112751-women-read-labels-more)). Sugar alcohols such as maltitol, which most high-protein bars rely on, are a well-known cause of gas and bloating ([Poison.org](https://poison.org/articles/do-protein-bars-give-you-gas)). That gives a concrete, believable promise: "no sugar alcohols, no sweeteners". The risks with B are that these women buy bars less often, are harder to find in large numbers locally, and may find heavy pastel styling too young for them. One thing worth testing is the age group in between. The Grocer reports that **25–34s are the most engaged with healthy snacking** ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/analysis-and-features/nutritional-needs-taste-perceptions-and-natural-protein-10-charts-explaining-uk-attitudes-to-healthy-snacking/605136.article)), and a "gentle, real-food" message may reach down into the late twenties and thirties as well.

## Every 15g+ bar uses additives, which leaves a 10g clean gap

The competitor evidence is the most decision-useful part of this research. **No mainstream UK bar was found that delivers 10g+ of protein with no sweeteners, polyols, emulsifiers or chicory inulin.** Brands either accept lower protein or accept additives. Barebells (20g) uses maltitol, sucralose and acesulfame K ([Barebells UK](https://barebells.co.uk/product/barebells-salty-peanut/)). Grenade Carb Killa carries around 14g of maltitol per bar on one listing and a polyol laxative warning ([MyFoodData](https://tools.myfooddata.com/nutrition-facts/101401158/wt1)). Vieve, which looks aimed at young women, uses chicory and polydextrose ([Gymfluencers](https://gymfluencers.com/unwrapping-the-new-vieve-protein-bar/)). So the young gym-goer currently has no additive-free option at all. She also has not shown that she wants one badly enough to give up 10g of protein for it.

The midlife, gut-health space is already being claimed, and that matters. **Bio&Me Daily Boost** (gut-health dietitian Dr Megan Rossi) is aimed squarely at women, is sold in Tesco, Sainsbury's, Waitrose and Holland & Barrett, has no sweeteners and gives **9.2g protein and 8g fibre in 40g**. However, it uses **chicory root fibre (inulin)**, a common trigger for people with IBS ([Sainsbury's](https://www.sainsburys.co.uk/groceries/product/bio-me-daily-boost-3-x-40g-fibre-protein-cocoa-flapjack-bars); [FoodBev](https://www.foodbev.com/news/bio-me-launches-new-protein-and-fibre-bars-targeting-female-consumers)). **ZOE's Gut Health Bar** gives 7g protein and 8g fibre at about **£2.33** but is sold only direct to customers ([ZOE](https://zoe.com/en-gb/buyguthealthbar); [NutraIngredients](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/06/25/zoe-targets-fiber-gap-with-new-gut-health-bar)). Hormone-focused bars are also arriving. **Fem**, a period-nutrition bar at £2.25 per 40g, won **450 Holland & Barrett stores** in 2026 ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/period-nutrition-bar-secures-holland-and-barrett-listing/721686.article)). Menopause has only a tiny subscription brand, Mena, at about £2.50 a bar ([Mena](https://www.mena-pause.co.uk/products/mena-protein-bars-1-1)).

That leaves a clear gap: **a bar for women with 10g or more of protein from recognisable ingredients, 5g or more of fibre without inulin, no sweeteners or sugar alcohols, a fudgy texture that feels like a treat, priced at or below about £2.50.** Bio&Me has the expert credibility and the shelf space, and could reformulate to close the gap. Ewan's edge would have to be beating Bio&Me on protein and on being genuinely gentle (no chicory), and beating ZOE on price and availability. That is a sharper and more defensible story than "natural protein bar for gym girls", which goes straight up against Barebells' taste.

## The rules shape the product as much as the customer does

Several UK rules apply whichever segment Ewan chooses. Two of them change what the bar can say on the front of the pack.

**Protein claims depend on calories, not grams.** Under the GB nutrition-claim rules, "source of protein" requires at least **12% of energy** from protein and "high protein" at least **20%**. "High fibre" requires **6g per 100g** ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/cy/eur/2006/1924/annex); [University of Reading](https://reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/nutrition-claims.htm)). Nuts and dates are calorie-dense, so a bar can be rich in protein and still fail the test. Peanut butter, at about 27g protein per 100g, does not qualify as "high protein" ([Rhitrition](https://www.rhitrition.com/articles/how-to-combat-misleading-labelling)). As a rough worked example (not from a source), a bar at 450 kcal per 100g needs about **22.5g protein per 100g** for "high protein", which is about 9g in a 40g bar. That is at the edge of what a clean recipe can manage. "Source of protein" (about 13.5g per 100g) is easy. **Dates also rule out "no added sugar"**: the ASA ruled against a "0g added sugar" claim where fruit concentrates were used to sweeten ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/keeping-the-regulator-sweet.html)).

**"Natural", "gut health" and "no additives" all need careful wording.** Under FSA guidance enforced through the ASA, a bar made of several ingredients cannot be called "natural" itself. It can say "made from natural ingredients" only if every ingredient qualifies, and enzyme-processed ingredients have failed that test before ([ASA Appy ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/appy-food---drinks-ltd-a16-348064.html)). "Gut health", "prebiotic" and "probiotic" count as general health claims. They are allowed only when placed right next to a specific authorised claim, and the ASA has upheld complaints against Kellogg and others ([CAP](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/food-probiotic-claims.html); [ASA Kellogg](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/kellogg-europe-trading-ltd-a22-1158376-kellogg-europe-trading-ltd.html)). The few authorised gut claims are dry, for example "rye fibre contributes to normal bowel function" ([Radiocentre](https://www.radiocentre.org/copy-guidelines/gut-health/)). "Gentle on the gut" probably needs the same treatment, so check it with a regulatory adviser before printing packaging. "No additives" has to be literally true, including "carry-over" additives: an additive allowed in an ingredient can end up in the finished bar through that ingredient ([University of Reading](https://reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/carry-over.htm)). Lecithin in chocolate chips or sulphites in dried fruit would make the claim false. Get written specifications from every supplier.

**The bar is likely to be classed as HFSS (high in fat, salt or sugar), which limits paid advertising.** From **5 January 2026**, paid online adverts for identifiable "less healthy" products are banned, and in-store rules on location and multibuy deals already apply in England ([Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmgi1iilt00r2v9ngngn5x1vu/food-for-thought-navigating-the-uk-s-hfss-advertising-ban); [Shoosmiths](https://www.shoosmiths.com/insights/articles/hfss-volume-price-promotion-restrictions-for-less-healthy-food-and-drink-are-coming)). The score is worked out per 100g using the government's nutrient-profiling model: points for calories, saturated fat, sugar and salt, minus points for fruit, vegetables and nuts, fibre and protein ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/reports-and-data/how-the-nutrient-profiling-model-works-as-simple-as-a-minus-c/672274.article)). The researcher's rough, unverified scoring suggests a sweetened protein bar would land well into HFSS territory. A bar that is at least 80% fruit and nuts might pass, but adding protein powder dilutes that share. This matters more for Segment A, which is reached mainly through paid social and TikTok. Businesses with fewer than 50 staff are exempt from the promotion rules, but the sources disagree on whether small firms are exempt from the online advertising ban. **Check this before spending a penny on paid adverts**, and run an NPM score on every prototype.

**The product can be made, but the protein target has to be modest.** Without glycerol or sugar alcohols, high-protein bars harden over time through moisture movement and browning reactions ([Massey University](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/6c5684a2-a0af-43b6-8520-fc49baa9477b/content); [University of Minnesota](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/maillard-reaction-induced-modification-and-aggregation-of-protein/)). The researcher's estimate is that about **15–22% protein by weight, or 6–10g in a 40–45g bar**, is achievable additive-free with a soft texture. The 30%+ protein of mainstream bars probably is not. That estimate is an informed inference, not a measured figure. Some suppliers sell "soft bar" milk proteins that they describe as free of additives, such as Arla's TexturePro and Carbery's Optipep ([Baking Business](https://www.bakingbusiness.com/articles/47394-texture-assist-from-whey); [Carbery](https://www.carbery.com/?p=1320)), but their claims have not been independently checked. Shelf stability comes from keeping the bar dry inside (water activity below about 0.6–0.7) and using good barrier packaging ([AQUALAB](https://aqualab.com/learn/seven-steps-ensuring-safe-consistent-cold-pressed-bars)). Psyllium husk and oat fibre are ordinary ingredients, not additives. Guar gum is an additive (E412), and its gut-friendly derivative PHGG is made with enzymes, which weakens a "natural" claim ([Yoon et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2212352)). There is one real design choice to make. **Dates are the classic "natural" sweetener and binder, but they are high-FODMAP**, meaning they can trigger bloating in sensitive people. A truly gut-gentle bar would lean on maple or rice syrup, which still count as sugar. Low-FODMAP certification exists (Monash charges an AUD 20 pre-assessment fee, then quotes for lab tests and licences) ([Monash](https://monashfodmap.com/product-and-recipe-certification-program/certify-a-product/)), but it is a later step, not a six-week one.

## At about £2.50, the economics favour selling direct first

Shelf prices set the ceiling. Grenade sells at **£2.90** in Sainsbury's ([Trolley](https://www.trolley.co.uk/grocery-price-index/brand_category_store/?brand=Grenade&category=Protein+Bars&store_id=3)), Grenade Soft Core at **£2.95** ([Grocery Gazette](https://www.grocerygazette.co.uk/2026/07/20/grenade-launches-soft-core-protein-bar-range/)) and Fulfil at **£2.90** for a single bar or about £2.40 in a multipack ([Tesco](https://www.tesco.com/shop/en-GB/products/308485599)). The women's functional bars sit lower, at £2.25–£2.50. A recommended retail price of **about £2.50 for a 40–45g bar** is defensible for Segment B. It is harder to justify to students.

The money left after retailers take their cut is thin. UK retailers typically take **30–45% of the shelf price excluding VAT**, wholesalers take another 15–30%, and the margins stack on top of each other ([GetGross](https://www.getgross.co.uk/guides/retailer-margin)). The researcher's illustrative calculation: at £2.50 with 20% VAT, the brand receives about **£1.25 selling direct to a shop, or about £0.94 through a wholesaler**. That means the full delivered cost of each bar probably needs to be around **£0.50–0.70** to leave a healthy margin after promotions. Clean nuts and soft proteins make that hard at small volumes. VAT makes a big difference: cereal bars are often zero-rated, but chocolate-coated bars are standard-rated ([GetGross](https://www.getgross.co.uk/guides/retailer-margin)). Avoiding a chocolate coating could add around 40p per bar to what the brand receives. Confirm the treatment with HMRC. Factory minimum orders range from about **1,000–3,000 bars per flavour** using a manufacturer's base recipe in custom packaging, to **5,000–30,000 bars** for a bespoke recipe ([Suppliers.ai](https://suppliers.ai/blog/best-private-label-protein-bar-manufacturers); [Wonnda](https://wonnda.com/product-types/protein-bar-manufacturers/)). No UK cost-per-bar quote was found.

The route to market differs by segment. Segment A is reached through TikTok Shop, where Lidl's £5 protein bundles proved demand ([Lidl](https://corporate.lidl.co.uk/media-centre/pressreleases/2025/tiktok-shop-protein)), through gyms and through micro-influencers costing **£300–1,200 per TikTok video** ([Gigapay](https://www.gigapay.com/blog/uk-influencer-rates-2026)). The HFSS advertising ban, however, threatens the paid side of that playbook. Segment B's natural path is the one Fem, Native Snacks and Battle Oats followed: start direct and in independent shops, then move to Ocado and Holland & Barrett ([The Grocer on Native](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/native-snacks-rebrands-ahead-of-sainsburys-and-holland-and-barrett-launch/652283.article); [Eat Drink Yorkshire](https://eatdrinkyorkshire.co.uk/hull-based-nutrition-brand-supply-300-holland-barrett-stores/)). Faire takes **25% on first orders and 15% on reorders**, but 0% on shops the brand brings to the platform itself ([Craftybase](https://api.craftybase.com/blog/how-much-does-faire-charge)). Two programmes are worth applying to straight away. Sainsbury's relaunched Future Brands in September 2026 with a **"Feel Good convenience"** track ([Grocery Gazette](https://www.grocerygazette.co.uk/2026/09/17/sainsburys-launches-future-brands-programme/)). FADNE, with Durham County Council as a partner, runs a fully funded North East food business support programme open to start-ups ([FADNE](https://www.fadne.org/business-support/)).

## A six-week test that ends with a decision

The research leaves one big question open: will midlife women actually pay £2.50, again and again, for a bar with about 8–10g of protein? Desk research cannot answer that, so the remaining weeks should produce primary evidence from both segments using the same product and the same price. The pass/fail thresholds below are suggested rules of thumb, not figures from the research. Agree them with the co-founder before Week 1, so that the result settles the question instead of starting an argument.

| Week | What to do | Segment A (20–26, gym) | Segment B (35–55, health-conscious) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make 2 kitchen-batch recipes (one date-sweetened, one maple/rice-syrup "low-FODMAP style"), aiming for 8–10g protein and 5g+ fibre per 40–45g. Calculate each recipe's HFSS score and claims eligibility. Build two simple landing pages with different messages and the same £2.50 price. | Message: "the sweet treat that's actually good for you: real protein, no fake sweeteners" | Message: "real-food protein and fibre, no sweeteners, no inulin, kind to your gut" |
| 2 | Blind taste tests with at least 30 people per segment, rating against a Barebells and a Bio&Me bar (anonymised). Score taste, texture, fullness and purchase intent at £2.50. | Durham Uni gyms, sports and Pilates societies, college JCRs | Local Pilates/yoga studios, parents of friends, workplace groups, menopause and IBS Facebook groups, a farm shop stall |
| 3 | 3-day home-use trial with 10–15 people per segment, then 20-minute interviews: digestion, when they ate it, what it replaced, what they would pay. | Note how often protein grams come up | Note how often bloating and ingredients come up |
| 4 | Real-money test: pre-orders or deposits for a 10-bar box (around £22–25) via each landing page. Promote through organic social, community posts and gifting only until the HFSS/SME advertising position is confirmed. | TikTok/Instagram from 5–10 gym and Pilates nano-creators | Facebook groups, women's-health newsletters, Instagram |
| 5 | Supply side: get 3 co-manufacturer quotes (minimum order, price at minimum and at double), a lab nutrition and water-activity estimate, and a 15-minute regulatory check on "gentle on the gut", "no additives" and the SME advertising exemption. Ask 5 local stockists (gym café, Pilates studio, farm shop, independent health shop, Fenwick food hall) whether they would list it, and at what margin. | Gym and campus outlets | Health shops, farm shops, Fenwick |
| 6 | Decide using the pre-agreed rules, then prepare the Sainsbury's Future Brands and FADNE applications around the winning segment. | | |

Suggested decision rules: choose the segment with the **higher share of "definitely/probably would buy at £2.50" (aim for 40%+)**, the **higher landing-page-to-pre-order conversion**, and **at least one stockist saying yes**. If Segment A wins on intent but only at a lower price, or only with more protein than a clean recipe can deliver, treat that as a sign the brand would be pulled away from its "additive-free" promise. If both segments do similarly, the reasoning above favours B, because its price headroom and lower ad dependence make the business easier to run. Track two cheap extra signals as well. First, the age of anyone in their late twenties or thirties who converts, since they may be the real sweet spot. Second, whether the pastel branding reads as "premium" or "too young" to over-40s.

## Conclusion

The most useful shift in thinking is that the choice of segment is mostly decided by the product, not by the size of each market. Insisting on no additives caps protein at a level that is mid-table for a gym-goer but leading for a midlife woman. It also creates a "gentle on the gut" promise that matters most to the group with the most bloating. Seen this way, the real competition for Segment B is Bio&Me and ZOE, not Barebells, and the realistic wedge is "more protein, no inulin, cheaper than ZOE". The HFSS advertising rules add a further, underrated reason to avoid a launch built on paid TikTok adverts. Two caveats are important. First, the evidence base is thin where it matters most: there is no current UK data on willingness to pay, or on bar buying, split by age and gender, and much of what exists is old, paywalled or commissioned by brands. Second, an established incumbent could close the gap by reformulating. The six-week test is therefore not a formality.
