## What this means
**Definitely target first (beachhead): competing amateur MMA fighters at your gym and through your channel.**
- **Highest need:** they cut weight (~97%, X1), train twice a day, and under-eat carbs (X3).
- **Highest reach:** your gym and your 25k MMA channel.
- **Direct demand:** your 8 fighters asked for exactly this.
- **Weakness:** small (~5–7k active competitors in the UK and Ireland). That's fine for a first market, but not the end game.

**Then expand to (same gyms, same need):**
1. **Women in combat sports.** Fast-growing (England Boxing women +23.5% in two years), they fight in weight classes too, they're underserved by male-coded brands, and women told you they'd consider a real-food bar.
2. **Muay Thai / kickboxing and BJJ.** Many MMA gyms already teach both, so it's the same gym and the same fridge. Weight classes at competitions.
3. **MMA gym members who don't compete.** Bigger, but lower need (some say current bars are fine).

**One to watch: white-collar boxers.** These are adults with money, training hard for 8 weeks to a target weight, often for the first time, and wanting guidance. A **"camp box" sold per event** could work well. Promoter numbers only, so verify.

**Lower priority:** competitive amateur boxers (many are juniors on low budgets), university clubs (small), and recreational boxing fitness (low need).

## Biggest gaps (fill these to make the case stronger)
1. **A real UK MMA count.** Ask **Safe MMA / English MMA** how many amateurs are registered. That would be a strong, official number for the pitch.
2. **Counts from your conversations:** how many of the 8 fighters said current bars are "fine", and how many women you spoke to.
3. **Sport England Active Lives tables** for combat sports (free download, or Durham library for Statista).
4. **England Boxing North East numbers:** ask the Tyne, Tees and Wear association for local clubs and competitive boxers.
5. **Spend:** what fighters spend on food and supplements in a fight camp (ask in interviews; no UK data found).
