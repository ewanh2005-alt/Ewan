## One-page summary
- **Size:**
  - About **7.2 million UK women are aged 45–60** `[EVIDENCE: W2, ONS, mid-2019]`, and NICE describes about **13 million women over 45** `[EVIDENCE: W1]`.
  - On national activity rates, roughly **4.4 million** are "active", about **170,000 in the North East** and **35,000 in County Durham** `[ASSUMPTION-based estimate, script]`.
  - Locally, that's a much bigger pool than university athletes.
- **Real need, backed by science:** protein matters more with age.
  - The expert recommendation for older adults is **1.0–1.2 g protein per kg per day**, more if active `[EVIDENCE: W4, PROT-AGE]`.
  - Low protein and low activity are the main drivers of muscle and strength loss in women after menopause `[EVIDENCE: W5]`.
  - UK women eat **far below the 30 g a day fibre target** `[EVIDENCE: W6]`.
  - Ultra-processed food makes up over half of UK adults' energy intake `[EVIDENCE: S30]`.
- **Interest in the category is growing:**
  - Women are now **51% of people trying to eat more protein** worldwide `[EVIDENCE: W9]`.
  - **60% of UK shoppers** say they cut back on ultra-processed food in the past year `[EVIDENCE: W10]`.
  - **58% of UK adults** say whether a food is ultra-processed affects what they choose `[EVIDENCE: W11]`.
- **But it's not clearly a "migraine" problem.**
  - For athletes, the pain is daily and urgent: needing to refuel after training.
  - For midlife women, it's a **long-term health goal** (muscle, bone, weight, energy), which tends to feel more like a "vitamin" than a painkiller `[ASSUMPTION: needs interviews]`.
- **The competition is already here.**
  - **Bio&Me Fibre + Protein bars** target **women aged 35–45**: 40 g bar, ~9 g protein, ~7.6 g fibre, 5–6 g natural sugar, no sweeteners, about **£1.40**, in Tesco, Waitrose and Holland & Barrett `[EVIDENCE: W12, W13]`.
  - **Mena**, a UK menopause food range, includes snack bars `[EVIDENCE: W14]`.
- **The biggest gaps:**
  - no UK data on what these women spend on snacks, or what they'd pay
  - no data on their snacking pain points
  - **no first-hand interviews**

**Provisional recommendation: keep athletes as the main market, and test women aged 40–60 as a second market.** Do 5–8 interviews and run the Meta ad split test before deciding (see the scorecard).

## 2. Nutrition needs (science, not marketing)
- **Protein:**
  - The PROT-AGE expert group recommends **≥1.0–1.2 g per kg per day** for adults over 65, and **≥1.2 g per kg** for those who exercise `[EVIDENCE: W4]`.
  - A similar 1.0–1.2 g per kg figure for women over 50 is attributed to ESCEO, but I couldn't check the original `[EVIDENCE: W4b, secondary; verify]`.
  - **Studies on women in midlife specifically are limited** `[EVIDENCE: W4b]`.
- **Menopause, muscle and strength:** low physical activity and low protein intake are the **biggest contributors** to muscle loss and strength loss in women after menopause `[EVIDENCE: W5, 2010 review]`. Strength training plus enough protein is the consistent advice `[EVIDENCE: W4, W5]`.
- **Fibre:**
  - UK adults average about **15–21 g a day** (NDNS years 7–8, AOAC method).
  - UK women aged 19–64 averaged about **13 g a day** on the older NSP method.
  - The target is **30 g** `[EVIDENCE: W6]`.
  - Only about 4% of adults meet it `[EVIDENCE: 01d, NDNS]`.
- **Sugar:** free sugar intakes are well above the 5%-of-energy limit across all age groups `[EVIDENCE: W6]`.
- **Ultra-processed food:** about 54% of UK adults' energy comes from it `[EVIDENCE: S30]`.

**What this means for fettle:** a snack with about 15 g protein and fibre, from real food and low in sugar, fits these needs. Don't make menopause health claims. Only authorised claims, such as "high protein", are allowed `[EVIDENCE: R-claims]`.

## 3. Behaviour and motivations
- **Interest in protein is shifting towards women:** women are 51% of consumers trying to boost their protein intake `[EVIDENCE: W9, Euromonitor 2025]`.
- **The Grocer** reports a "rapid influx of female consumers" into protein bars, which used to be male-dominated `[EVIDENCE: W9b]`.
- **Ultra-processed food:**
  - 73% of UK adults are aware of the term, and 58% say it affects their choices `[EVIDENCE: W11, Robinson et al. 2024, n=2,386, 50% female]`.
  - 60% of UK shoppers say they cut back in the past year `[EVIDENCE: W10, GlobalData 2025]`.
- **Fresh delivery:** Gousto's customers skew towards **women aged 35–50 cooking for families** `[EVIDENCE: W15, Gousto exec, qualitative]`, so weekly food delivery is familiar to this group `[ASSUMPTION for fettle]`.
- **Snacking:**
  - Women are more likely than men to reach for fruit as a healthy snack (43% vs 32%) `[EVIDENCE: W16, undated survey; weak]`.
  - Convenience matters more than indulgence in the afternoon `[EVIDENCE: W16b]`.
  - I found no recent UK data specific to women aged 45–54 `[GAP]`.
- **Where they get advice:** I found no reliable UK data. Menopause influencers, GPs and Mumsnet are likely `[GAP]`.

## 4. Pain points with current options
- **Sugar alcohols** (maltitol, erythritol) in protein bars commonly cause bloating and diarrhoea `[EVIDENCE: W17, diabetes forum + articles; weak but consistent]`.
- **Artificial sweeteners** can leave a bitter or metallic aftertaste `[EVIDENCE: W17b]`.
- **Protein bars were male-oriented** (gym and supplement image) `[EVIDENCE: W9b]`.
- **No Mumsnet threads found** in the search. Direct forum research is needed `[GAP]`. No quotes were invented.

**Where's the gap?**
- **Bio&Me is close.** It's real food, women-focused and cheap. Its weak spots:
  - only about **9 g protein**
  - its protein comes partly from oats and seeds, so it's not high-protein
- **fettle v6** offers about **15 g protein per 50 g from egg, quark and oats**, with no whey or sweeteners.
- **The gap is "high protein + real food + low sugar for women".** The catch: fettle is fresh and chilled, and would cost more (about £2–2.75 vs £1.40).

**Verdict (provisional):** athletes stay the main market. Women 40–60 is a **credible second market** with a bigger pool and a real nutritional need, but a weaker sense of urgency and an existing competitor. Decide with primary evidence.
