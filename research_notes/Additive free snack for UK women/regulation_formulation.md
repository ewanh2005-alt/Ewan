# UK Regulation and Formulation Feasibility: Additive-free, Protein/Fibre, Gut-friendly Sweet Snack Bar (GB, as of Oct 2026)

Many findings below therefore come from search-result summaries of those pages, ASA pages, law-firm briefings and journal abstracts, not from full-text reading. Where I relied on established regulatory knowledge that I could not re-verify with a source this session, I put it under "Inferences" (marked "unverified") or "Gaps". ## 1. GB nutrition claim thresholds (retained Reg. 1924/2006 Annex)

### Takeaway
The protein claims are based on energy, not weight: "source of protein" needs ≥12% of energy from protein and "high protein" needs ≥20%. Fibre claims are based on weight or energy: "source" is ≥3 g/100 g, "high" is ≥6 g/100 g or ≥3 g/100 kcal. A calorie-dense nut and date bar can therefore miss "high protein" even with a lot of protein per 100 g. "No added sugars" rules out dates or fruit concentrates used to sweeten.

### Cited Findings
- "Source of protein" requires at least 12% of the food's energy from protein. "High protein" requires at least 20% of energy from protein. — [legislation.gov.uk, Reg 1924/2006 Annex](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/cy/eur/2006/1924/annex); [Univ. of Reading food law – nutrition claims](https://reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/nutrition-claims.htm)
- "High fibre" requires at least 6 g fibre/100 g or at least 3 g/100 kcal. — [legislation.gov.uk Annex](https://legislation.gov.uk/eur/2006/1924/annexes/2006-12-20); [EU Commission nutrition claims page](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/labelling-and-nutrition/nutrition-and-health-claims/nutrition-claims_en)
- Energy-dense foods can fail the protein thresholds despite high protein per 100 g. For example, peanut butter has about 27 g protein/100 g and still fails "high protein". — [Rhitrition, misleading labelling](https://www.rhitrition.com/articles/how-to-combat-misleading-labelling)
- "Source of fibre" alternatives: a Trading Standards Business Companion sheet (Feb 2025, with a Sep 2026 edition also listed) is reported in search summaries as giving "3 g/100 g or 1 g per 100 calories". The original EU annex gives 1.5 g/100 kcal. **Conflict / possible summarisation error. Verify directly.** — [Business Companion permitted nutrition claims Sep 2026 (PDF)](https://www.businesscompanion.info/sites/default/files/Nutrition-and-health-claims-Permitted-nutrition-claims-Sep-2026.pdf); [Business Companion Feb 2025 (PDF)](https://businesscompanion.info/sites/default/files/Nutrition-and-health-claims-Permitted-nutrition-claims-Feb-2025.pdf)
- "With no added sugars" is permitted only where no added mono- or disaccharides **or any other food used for its sweetening properties** have been added. The ASA ruled against Trip Drink's "0g added sugar" claim because grape and apple concentrates sweetened the product. The ASA assesses sweetening purpose case by case: the nature of the product, why each ingredient is used, labelling and name. — [ASA "Keeping the regulator sweet"](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/keeping-the-regulator-sweet.html)
- Dutch self-regulator rulings under the same EU text upheld complaints against "no added sugar" claims where date paste, apple juice concentrate or beet syrup were used. UK brand Urban Fruit was told to remove the claim from apple-juice-sweetened sweets. — [FoodNavigator 2018](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2018/12/14/Kellogg-Hipp-and-five-others-told-to-remove-misleading-no-added-sugar-claims)

### Inferences
- (Unverified against the source this session, but this is the standard Annex text.) "Low sugars" means ≤5 g sugars/100 g for solids. "Sugars-free" means ≤0.5 g/100 g. "No added sugars" must carry the statement "contains naturally occurring sugars" where sugars are naturally present. "Low fat" means ≤3 g/100 g for solids. "Low saturated fat" means saturated plus trans fat ≤1.5 g/100 g and ≤10% of energy. **Practical consequence:** a sweet-treat bar sweetened with dates, maple or rice syrup will almost never meet "low sugar". It also cannot say "no added sugars" if dates or syrups are there to sweeten.
- Worked example (inference): a 450 kcal/100 g bar needs ≥22.5 g protein/100 g (≥90 kcal) for "high protein" and ≥13.5 g/100 g for "source of protein". "High fibre" (≥6 g/100 g) is achievable with oats, nuts and psyllium.
- The GB claim conditions are still the same as the EU Annex as far as I could find. Northern Ireland follows EU law under the Windsor Framework, so EU Register changes apply there and not in GB.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the Business Companion Sep 2026 PDF to confirm the "source of fibre" per-energy figure or whether GB has amended any thresholds.

## 2. Authorised health claims (protein, fibre, calcium, vitamin D, magnesium) and "gut health", probiotic and prebiotic claims

### Takeaway
Only claims on the GB NHC Register can be used. "Gut health", "probiotic" and "prebiotic" are treated as general health claims, and the ASA has repeatedly upheld complaints against them. They are only permitted when immediately accompanied by a specific authorised claim. Very few gut-related claims are authorised, and their wording is unappealing (e.g. "rye fibre contributes to normal bowel function").

### Cited Findings
- Since 1 January 2021, only claims on the GB NHC Register are permitted in UK food marketing. General health claims must be accompanied by a specific authorised claim, and "accompanied" means next to or immediately following it. — [CAP advice: food probiotic claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/food-probiotic-claims.html)
- In the Willy's ruling, the ASA held that "probiotic" is understood as contributing to general gut health and is therefore a general health claim. In the Heights rulings, linking probiotics to immunity or mood was a general claim with no authorised backing, so it was banned. — [CAP advice: probiotic claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/food-probiotic-claims.html); [Osborne Clarke on Heights](https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/advertising-regulation/trust-your-gut-instincts-check-that-your-specific-health-claims-meet-the-gb-register-requirements/)
- In the Kellogg ruling, "prebiotic" references implying overall gut health were general health claims. A prebiotic claim tied to a specific benefit would be a specific claim and would need authorisation. — [ASA Kellogg ruling](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/kellogg-europe-trading-ltd-a22-1158376-kellogg-europe-trading-ltd.html)
- Very few authorised gut-related claims exist and they are not consumer-friendly (e.g. "rye fibre contributes to normal bowel function"). Radiocentre knows of no authorised gut-health disease-risk-reduction claims. — [Radiocentre copy guidelines: gut health](https://www.radiocentre.org/copy-guidelines/gut-health/)
- Authorised "contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue" claims exist for listed nutrients, including magnesium. — [ASA ruling Nature's Best](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/natures-best-health-products-ltd-a11-179089.html); [EFSA opinion magnesium](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/1807)
- To make a calcium health claim, the product must contain at least 15% of the reference intake (NRV), i.e. be a "source". — [BNF "Know your labels"](https://nutrition.org.uk:443/media/pjkjxips/know-your-labels_nutrition-and-health-claims.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Unverified this session. Standard Reg 432/2012 wording, retained in GB.)
  - Protein claims ("contributes to a growth in muscle mass", "...maintenance of muscle mass", "...maintenance of normal bones") need the food to meet "source of protein" (≥12% energy).
  - Fibre-specific claims with their conditions: oat grain fibre or wheat bran fibre "contributes to an increase in faecal bulk" requires "high fibre". Oat/barley beta-glucan cholesterol claims require 1 g beta-glucan per portion.
  - Calcium ("needed for maintenance of normal bones"), vitamin D ("contributes to normal function of the immune system"/"bones") and magnesium ("reduction of tiredness and fatigue", "normal muscle function") all need ≥15% NRV per 100 g.
- **Additive-free tension:** reaching 15% NRV for calcium (120 mg/100 g), magnesium (56 mg/100 g) or vitamin D (0.75 µg/100 g) usually needs added mineral salts or vitamins. Calcium is the possible exception, from milk proteins and almonds. Magnesium may also come from nuts, seeds and cacao. Added vitamins and minerals are not legally "additives", but consumers may see them as contradicting a "nothing artificial" message, and a "natural" claim could be challenged. Vitamin D at meaningful levels is unrealistic without fortification. Mushroom vitamin D is a possible clean-label route, but I did not check its novel food status.
- Lawful gut messaging path (inference): use "high fibre" and pair any "supports gut health" line immediately with an authorised specific claim the product actually qualifies for, e.g. the oat grain fibre faecal bulk claim. Avoid "prebiotic" and "probiotic" unless a specific authorised claim backs them, and none does for generic prebiotic fibre in GB. "Gentle on the gut" or "low FODMAP" wording carries risk: it could be read as a general health claim, so it would need accompaniment. Low-FODMAP certification logos are widely used, but I found no ASA ruling on them.

### Gaps
- I could not open the GB NHC Register to confirm whether GB has added or removed any relevant claims since 2021 (e.g. new fibre or PHGG claims). I found no GB-authorised claim for PHGG or psyllium bowel function. In the EU there is no authorised psyllium claim on the general list. Unverified.

## 3. "No additives", "natural", "clean label" and "free from" claims; carry-over additives

### Takeaway
There is no statutory definition of "no additives", "natural" or "clean label". The FSA's 2008 criteria, enforced in practice via the ASA and Trading Standards under the general rule against misleading labelling, say a compound food such as a bar cannot itself be called "natural". It may say "made from natural ingredients" only if every ingredient meets the criteria. "No additives" must be literally true, including carry-over additives in compound ingredients.

### Cited Findings
- FSA "Criteria for the use of the terms fresh, pure, natural etc." (revised July 2008): unqualified "natural" is only for single traditional foods with nothing added. Compound foods should not be called natural directly or by implication, but "made from natural ingredients" is acceptable if all ingredients meet the criteria. Chemically formed colours and flavours make "natural" misleading. — [ASA ruling Appy Food & Drinks](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/appy-food---drinks-ltd-a16-348064.html); [BakeryandSnacks 2008](https://bakeryandsnacks.com/Article/2008/07/11/New-guidelines-aim-to-do-away-with-misleading-marketing-terms)
- The ASA upheld a "100% natural" complaint because calcium lactate and enzymatically produced glucose-fructose syrup do not meet the FSA "natural" criteria, since enzymatic treatment is non-traditional. — [FoodNavigator on ASA ruling](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2017/03/09/Stop-claiming-calcium-lactate-and-glucose-fructose-syrup-are-natural-ingredients-ASA-rules/); [ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/appy-food---drinks-ltd-a16-348064.html)
- The test is whether consumers are misled based on their expectations. Commentators note there is uncertainty in how the guidance applies. — [Bakery Info / Blake Morgan](https://bakeryinfo.co.uk/branding/marketing/legal-defining-the-term-natural/625025.article)
- Carry-over principle (Reg 1333/2008 Art 18): an additive allowed in an ingredient may be present in the final compound food through that ingredient. — [Univ. of Reading – carry-over](https://reading.ac.uk/foodlaw/label/carry-over.htm)

### Inferences
- (Unverified this session.) Under Food Information Reg 1169/2011 Art 20, carry-over additives with no technological function in the final food need not appear in the ingredients list. A bar could therefore contain, say, lecithin from chocolate chips or anticaking agents in a protein powder without it being visible on the label. A "no additives" claim would then be false and actionable. **Due diligence:** get specifications confirming "no additives" for every compound ingredient. Common culprits:
  - chocolate (soy or sunflower lecithin, E322)
  - protein powders (lecithin used for instantising)
  - dried fruit (sulphites E220–228, or rice flour/oil coatings)
  - coconut (sulphites)
  - flavourings, rice syrup solids, puffed crisps (emulsifiers, salt).
- (Unverified.) Food Information Reg Art 7(1)(c) bans implying special characteristics when all similar foods share them. "Free from X" or "no X" claims should be used only where X would normally be expected in the category, which is plausible for "no sweeteners" and "no palm oil" in protein bars.
- Safer wording (inference):
  - "no additives", if fully true including carry-over
  - "no sweeteners, no sugar alcohols, no emulsifiers or preservatives"
  - "made from natural ingredients", only if every ingredient meets the FSA criteria. Protein isolates, PHGG and rice syrup made with enzymes are arguable. Following the Appy logic, enzymatic processing is a known vulnerability.
  - Avoid "100% natural" on the pack front and "clean label" as a consumer claim.
- Isolated protein powders (whey/milk protein isolate, pea isolate) are ingredients, not additives. Their "natural" status under the FSA criteria is contestable because they are heavily processed (ultrafiltration, wet fractionation).

### Gaps
- I could not access FSA or Trading Standards guidance text on "no additives" or "free from" specifically. I did not find an ASA ruling on a "no additives" claim defeated by carry-over additives. That would be a useful targeted check on the ASA rulings database.

## 4. HFSS ("less healthy food") advertising and promotion rules in effect by 2026; NPM scoring of the bar; implications for a small brand

### Takeaway
In England, location restrictions (Oct 2022) and volume-price restrictions (Oct 2025) are in force. The 9pm TV/on-demand watershed and the ban on paid online ads for identifiable "less healthy" products became legally binding on 5 January 2026. A sugar-sweetened protein bar will very likely be HFSS (NPM score ≥4). A predominantly nut and fruit bar can sometimes pass, but only if fruit/veg/nut content is very high, which conflicts with high protein from isolates. Small businesses have meaningful exemptions.

### Cited Findings
- Advertising: industry was asked to comply voluntarily from 1 October 2025, and the rules became legally binding on 5 January 2026. They comprise a 9pm–5:30am watershed on TV and on-demand services and a total ban on paid-for online advertising of identifiable less healthy products. Brand-only ads are outside scope. The ASA enforces with Ofcom. — [Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmgi1iilt00r2v9ngngn5x1vu/food-for-thought-navigating-the-uk-s-hfss-advertising-ban); [Food Manufacture, CAP final guidance Dec 2025](https://www.foodmanufacture.co.uk/Article/2025/12/12/the-cap-has-published-its-final-guidance-ahead-of-januarys-lhf-ad-watershed-and-online-ban/); [MFMac Jan 2026](https://www.mfmac.com/insights/manufacturing-media-technology/the-uk-s-new-junk-food-advertising-ban/)
- SME exemption: food and drink businesses with fewer than 250 employees are reported as exempt from the TV/on-demand watershed but still subject to the online ban (per search summary of the sources above). **Possible conflict:** my recollection of the government's 2021 response is that SMEs are exempt from both TV and online restrictions. Verify against the Advertising (Less Healthy Food Definitions and Exemptions) Regulations 2024 and CAP guidance. — [Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmgi1iilt00r2v9ngngn5x1vu/food-for-thought-navigating-the-uk-s-hfss-advertising-ban)
- Scope: a product is "less healthy" if it is in a scheduled category **and** scores ≥4 (food) under the 2004/05 Nutrient Profiling Model. — [Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmgi1iilt00r2v9ngngn5x1vu/food-for-thought-navigating-the-uk-s-hfss-advertising-ban); [ASA – HFSS nutrient profiling](https://asa.org.uk/advice-online/food-hfss-nutrient-profiling.html)
- Promotions (England):
  - Location restrictions from 1 October 2022 cover checkouts, aisle ends, store entrances and online home, landing and checkout pages.
  - Volume-price restrictions (e.g. BOGOF, 3-for-2) took effect on 1 October 2025.
  - Both apply to 13 categories of pre-packed less healthy food. Meal deals are excluded and pre-packed multipacks are still allowed.
  - Businesses with fewer than 50 employees are exempt. Sources differ on whether location rules also depend on store floor area above 185.8 m².
  - [Shoosmiths](https://www.shoosmiths.com/insights/articles/hfss-volume-price-promotion-restrictions-for-less-healthy-food-and-drink-are-coming); [ACS – in force](https://www.acs.org.uk/news/hfss-promotional-restrictions-come-force-today); [Brent Council](https://www.brent.gov.uk/business/food-safety-and-hygiene/food-help-and-advice/food-promotion-and-placement); [Bromley guidance](https://bromley.gov.uk/leaflet/332638/3/733/d); [Foodmatters Live](https://foodmatterslive.com/article/government-guidance-hfss-legislation-implementation-guidance-published/)
- In July 2025 the government said it expected to repeal the volume-price and placement rules. However, on 25 March 2026 England launched a consultation (closed 17 June 2026) on switching the existing location, volume-price and advertising restrictions to the **2018 NPM**, which treats the volume rules as in force. I found no confirmation of a repeal or of the consultation outcome. — [Osborne Clarke July 2025](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/regulatory-outlook-july-2025-food-law); [IGD on 2026 consultation](https://www.igd.com/articles/consultation-launches-defining-the-future-of-hfss-policy/72840); [Food Manufacture Sept 2025](https://www.foodmanufacture.co.uk/Article/2025/09/03/government-announces-intention-to-repeal-hfss-promo-ban-so-what-can-we-expect-now/)
- Devolved nations: Scotland is introducing promotion restrictions in October 2026. Northern Ireland consulted from 29 July to 21 October 2026 on volume-price and placement rules and on which NPM (2004/05 or 2018) to use. — [IGD](https://www.igd.com/articles/consultation-launches-defining-the-future-of-hfss-policy/72840); [NI DoH consultation PDF](https://www.health-ni.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/doh-hfss-promotion-consultation-doc.pdf); [Lewis Silkin Aug 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/08/04/consultation-launched-on-restricting-promotions-of-hfss-products-in-northern-irel-102nf6k)
- NPM mechanics: everything is calculated per 100 g, regardless of serving. "A" points come from energy, saturated fat, total sugars and sodium. "C" points come from fruit/veg/nuts (FVN), fibre and protein. Score = A − C. If A points are high (≥11), protein points cannot be counted unless FVN scores the maximum. — [The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/reports-and-data/how-the-nutrient-profiling-model-works-as-simple-as-a-minus-c/672274.article); [Menusano guide](https://www.menusano.com/calculate-hfss-score-guide/); [ASA](https://asa.org.uk/advice-online/food-hfss-nutrient-profiling.html)

### Inferences
- Illustrative 2004/05 NPM scoring, computed from my knowledge of the point tables (unverified this session, approximate):
  - **Typical date + nut + protein bar** (≈420 kcal, 35 g sugars, 4 g saturated fat, 0.2 g salt per 100 g). A points: energy ≈5, sugars ≈7, saturated fat ≈4, sodium ≈0, total ≈16. Because A ≥11, protein only counts if FVN content is ≥80%.
    - With FVN <80% (likely once 20–30% protein isolate is added), C is roughly fibre 5 plus FVN 0–2, so the score is about 9–11. **HFSS.**
    - A pure date-nut "Nakd-style" bar with ≥80% fruit/nuts gets FVN 5 + fibre 5 + protein 5 = 15, so the score is about 1–3. **Non-HFSS.** Caution: reported guidance treats dried fruit specially in FVN calculations. Verify.
  - **Sugar- or syrup-sweetened protein bar** with chocolate coating: sugars and saturated fat push A to ≥18. Almost certainly HFSS.
  - The proposed 2018 NPM is stricter on sugar, so more bars would become HFSS if adopted.
- Category scope: the regulated categories include "sweet biscuits and bars based on cereals" and "confectionery" (unverified wording). Whether a cereal-free nut, date and protein bar is in scope depends on the guidance's category descriptions, and I could not confirm treatment of protein bars. Most retailers apply the rules conservatively.
- Implications for a small brand:
  - With fewer than 50 staff, the brand's own DTC promotions are exempt.
  - Products sold through large retailers are bound by that retailer's location and volume restrictions. If HFSS, the bar cannot get gondola ends, checkout placement or multibuys.
  - Paid online product ads (Instagram, TikTok, search) for an HFSS bar are likely restricted from January 2026, depending on the SME carve-out (verify). Brand-only advertising and owned media, such as the brand's own website and organic social, remain options.
  - Designing the bar to score ≤3 (high FVN, high fibre, moderate sugar) is a real commercial advantage. However, it trades off against high protein from isolates, which lowers FVN %.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm the exact category list wording for bars, the precise SME advertising exemption, the outcome of the March 2026 England NPM consultation, or how dried fruit counts towards FVN.

## 5. Low-FODMAP certification (Monash, FODMAP Friendly): process and cost

### Takeaway
Both schemes involve lab testing of the finished product plus a licence agreement and on-pack logo. Neither publishes full fees. Monash charges a published pre-assessment fee of AUD 20 (USD 16) per product, then quotes for lab testing and licence fees. FODMAP Friendly describes a five-step, 4–6 week process with no public product price.

### Cited Findings
- Monash product certification: AUD 20 ex GST (USD 16) pre-assessment fee per product. Lab testing and licence fees depend on the number of products and are quoted via certification@monashfodmap.com. The programme lists product testing, consultation, agreement and licence fees separately. — [Monash – certify a product](https://monashfodmap.com/product-and-recipe-certification-program/certify-a-product/); [Monash certification flyer](https://MonashFODMAP.com/documents/19/Certification_Program_Flyer_.pdf); [Monash blog](https://monashfodmap.com/blog/monash-university-low-fodmap-certification-program-what-is-it-and-how-does-it-benefit-me/)
- Monash lab-tests finished products for FODMAP content. — [FODMAP Everyday – Monash lab testing explained](https://www.fodmapeveryday.com/monash-university-low-fodmap-lab-testing-explained)
- FODMAP Friendly: five steps (preliminary application, independent lab testing, licence agreement, artwork approval, ongoing marketing support), typically 4–6 weeks. The only public price found was USD 99/yr, which is for restaurant accreditation, not products. — [FODMAP Friendly FAQ – cost](https://fodmapfriendly.com/faqs/q-how-much-does-it-cost-to-get-accredited/)

### Inferences
- Budget a per-SKU lab test plus an annual licence fee (exact amounts unknown), and re-certification when the recipe changes. Certification constrains formulation: no dates, honey, agave, inulin/chicory root, apple/pear, cashews or pistachios, and limited quantities of some nuts and oats per serve. (FODMAP status of these ingredients is from Monash app knowledge and was not verified this session.)
- Both schemes are Australian-origin and operate internationally, including in the UK. I found no UK-specific scheme.

### Gaps
- No public UK pound pricing for either scheme. A direct quote is needed. There is also no evidence on whether the ASA treats a low-FODMAP logo as a health claim.

## 6. Formulation: sweetness, soft texture and shelf life without sweeteners, polyols, glycerol, emulsifiers or preservatives

### Takeaway
Feasible only as a cold-formed bar or bite. Sweetness and binding come from syrups or fruit pastes and nut butters, and water activity is kept below about 0.6–0.65 for mould stability. The low-FODMAP constraint removes dates, honey and agave, leaving rice syrup, maple syrup and sugar (sucrose) as the main sweet binders. All of these add sugar, which hurts "low sugar" and HFSS status.

### Cited Findings
- Cold-pressed bars are often made without chemical preservatives or large amounts of humectants. AQUALAB cites a food-code water activity (aw) limit of about 0.88 but recommends staying below 0.70 aw to prevent mould, and warns that meeting the code does not guarantee real shelf stability. — [AQUALAB – cold-pressed bars](https://aqualab.com/learn/seven-steps-ensuring-safe-consistent-cold-pressed-bars)
- Some gluten-free nut snack bars with aw below 0.6 had an estimated shelf life of only 49–58 days. That estimate was based on aw alone, and aw rose in storage because of poor barrier packaging. — [Revistas Chilenas study](https://revistaschilenas.uchile.cl/handle/2250/150710?show=full)
- Another bar with aw below 0.6 showed no microbial risk and an estimated 11-month shelf life at 25 °C, extrapolated from accelerated storage at 45 °C. — [OMU repository](https://acikerisim.omu.edu.tr/entities/publication/a0402d58-4655-47e2-88f1-03ab523263c6)
- In date bars, aw decreased during storage. — [FAO AGRIS – date bars](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65dfef1963b8185d9cb023fe)

### Inferences
- Realistic target: aw about 0.50–0.60, foil or metallised high-barrier flow-wrap, possibly nitrogen flush (inference). Typical commercial cold-formed fruit/nut bars carry roughly 6–12 months' shelf life (industry norm, not sourced this session). The limits will usually be rancidity of nut oils and texture hardening, not microbes. Without added antioxidants (e.g. tocopherols, rosemary extract, which count as additives or flavourings), choose fresh, low-PUFA nuts and use high-barrier packaging.
- Sweet binders compatible with low FODMAP (unverified FODMAP status): brown rice syrup (glucose/maltose; low FODMAP), maple syrup, small amounts of sucrose/coconut sugar, dark chocolate in small amounts (watch for lecithin), peanut or almond butter (in small portions), oats, rice crisps, seeds, cacao, cinnamon, vanilla.
  - Dates, honey, agave, apple juice concentrate and inulin are high FODMAP.
  - Rice syrup: heavy-metal (arsenic) awareness and glycaemic concerns. The glucose-rich syrup also accelerates Maillard browning with protein (see section 7).
- Baked vs cold-formed (inference): baking lowers aw and kills mould spores but increases Maillard reactions, protein denaturation and dryness. It also typically limits protein content, because high protein levels make baked goods tough. Cold-forming suits a soft, fudgy "treat" texture better. Bites (balls), possibly enrobed in chocolate, are a common format for clean-label products.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed shelf-life data found specifically for additive-free, low-FODMAP, cold-formed protein bars. Shelf life must be established by accelerated and real-time testing.

## 7. Protein bar hardening and realistic additive-free protein levels

### Takeaway
High-protein bars harden through moisture migration, protein aggregation (disulfide bonds) and Maillard cross-linking. The usual fixes are glycerol, polyols and specialised ingredients. Without them, the main levers are:
- lightly hydrolysed or "soft bar" milk proteins (which are ingredients, not additives)
- non-reducing or lower-reducing sugar systems
- fat and nut butter for plasticity
- low aw
- moderate protein targets.

### Cited Findings
- Contributing mechanisms include moisture migration, protein–water interactions, disulfide bonds, the Maillard reaction and sugar crystallisation. Polyphenols are proposed mitigators. — [Springer review 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44187-025-00541-0)
- Massey University research found hardening driven more by molecular mobility and microstructure changes from moisture migration than by chemical reactions. Water moves from high-osmotic components such as glucose and glycerol, and aggregated protein separates into phases. — [Massey thesis](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/6c5684a2-a0af-43b6-8520-fc49baa9477b/content); [Massey thesis 2](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/be7ab04c-32f6-40e9-b404-a0ab2111e226/content)
- Prolonged storage formed high-molecular-weight Maillard protein polymers that hardened bars. Replacing reducing sugars with sugar alcohols minimised the change. — [Univ. Minnesota, J Food Sci](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/maillard-reaction-induced-modification-and-aggregation-of-protein/)
- Maillard reactions run fastest at aw about 0.65–0.75. — [Massey thesis](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/6c5684a2-a0af-43b6-8520-fc49baa9477b/content)
- Reviewed mitigations: protein hydrolysates, protein modification, inhibiting disulfide bonding, reducing molecular migration, inhibiting Maillard reactions. — [Food Science (China) 2020 review](https://www.spgykj.com/en/article/doi/10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2020030289)
- Supplier "texture" proteins (vendor claims, not independent):
  - Carbery Optipep 4Bars, a lightly hydrolysed milk protein: 53% lower hardness at 12 months in a 30% protein bar at 7% inclusion. [Carbery](https://www.carbery.com/?p=1320)
  - Arla Lacprodan TexturePro: a whey ingredient said to soften bars, "contains no additives". [Baking Business](https://www.bakingbusiness.com/articles/47394-texture-assist-from-whey)
  - Fonterra SureProtein SoftBar 1000: a soft milk protein isolate, designed to be combined with texture builders. [Knowde/Fonterra](https://knowde.com/stores/nzmp-usa-fonterra-north-america/products/sureprotein-softbar-1000)
  - Gelita OPTIBAR: a collagen peptide binder, "free from E-numbers", suited to low-sugar bars, best combined with whey or plant protein. Note that collagen is an incomplete protein. [Gelita flyer](https://www.gelita.com/sites/default/files/documents/2025-04/OPTIBAR%20Folder.pdf); [FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/05/22/Gelita-launches-Optibar-for-soft-and-sugar-free-protein-bars/)

### Inferences
- Realistic protein levels without glycerol or polyols (inference, not sourced):
  - About 15–22% protein w/w (e.g. 6–10 g in a 40–45 g bite pack) is achievable with soft texture using nut butters, a portion of hydrolysed or soft milk protein, rice crisps and low aw.
  - Mainstream "high protein" bars at 30%+ rely on glycerol and polyols, so they are probably not achievable additive-free with acceptable 9–12-month texture.
  - At about 400–450 kcal/100 g, "high protein" (≥20% energy) needs about 20–23 g/100 g, which is borderline-achievable. "Source of protein" (≥12% energy) is easily achievable.
- Protein choices:
  - Whey isolate and milk protein isolate are low FODMAP because they are low in lactose. Whey protein concentrate carries more lactose. Milk protein harden fastest unless hydrolysed or specially processed.
  - Pea protein: earthy flavour, gritty, generally lower FODMAP as an isolate (unverified).
  - Egg white powder (RXBAR-style) is an additive-free option.
  - Collagen peptides soften but are nutritionally incomplete and not vegetarian.
- Sweetener choice affects hardening. Glucose-rich rice syrup and reducing sugars speed Maillard reactions. Sucrose (non-reducing) or maple (mainly sucrose) is likely better for texture stability, at the cost of a "refined sugar" perception.

### Gaps
- No independent data on hardening rates in polyol-free, glycerol-free bars at specific protein levels. Supplier figures are unverified.

## 8. Low-FODMAP, gut-friendly fibres (psyllium, PHGG, oat fibre) and whether they count as additives

### Takeaway
Psyllium husk and oat fibre are ordinary food ingredients, not additives. Guar gum is additive E412. PHGG is an enzymatically hydrolysed derivative that suppliers market as a dietary fibre ingredient, but its regulatory classification and "natural"/"no additives" defensibility needs confirmation. Inulin/chicory root fibre is high FODMAP and should be avoided.

### Cited Findings
- PHGG is produced enzymatically from guar gum. It has the same basic structure, but its chains are much shorter and it has very low viscosity (about 10 mPa·s at 5% vs 2,000–3,000 mPa·s for 1% guar). It is stable to heat, acid and digestion. It is sold as Sunfiber and Benefiber. — [Yoon et al., J Clin Biochem Nutr 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2212352)
- Guar gum is specified and labelled as E412. — [Polygal TDS](https://www.knowde.com:443/stores/polygal-ag/products/polygum-240-10); [EFSA 2024 re-evaluation of E412](https://arts.units.it/retrieve/2088528a-5bc9-4b47-ad56-f086a7948d8d/EFSA%20Journal%20-%202024%20-%20-%20Re%e2%80%90evaluation%20of%20guar%20gum%20E%20412%20as%20a%20food%20additive%20in%20foods%20for%20infants%20below%2016%20weeks%20of%20age.pdf)
- A dietitian education deck presents PHGG as a gut-tolerated fibre. — [Dietitian Connection PHGG presentation](https://dietitianconnection.com/app/uploads/2022/02/PHGG-presentation_JZhouV3.1.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Unverified.) Additive status depends on function. An ingredient used for its nutritional or fibre purpose is a food ingredient. One used for a technological purpose (thickening, binding) as a substance not normally eaten as a food is an additive.
  - Psyllium husk is consumed as a food and is labelled "psyllium husk". It is generally regarded as low FODMAP, and it also aids binding, a useful dual role.
  - Oat fibre is insoluble, low FODMAP and bland. Oat beta-glucan is an option if the cholesterol claim is wanted.
  - PHGG is labelled "partially hydrolysed guar gum" or "guar fibre" and is generally considered low FODMAP and well tolerated.
  - Enzymatic processing makes PHGG vulnerable under the FSA "natural" criteria (see the ASA Appy ruling).
- Psyllium absorbs water, which can firm the bar over time and needs aw management. Use moderate doses (about 2–4 g/100 g, inference) to avoid gritty or gummy texture and bloating.
- Avoid inulin, FOS and chicory root fibre (high FODMAP and gassy) and soluble corn fibre (check tolerance and processing).

### Gaps
- Could not confirm the GB novel-food or additive classification of PHGG, or authoritative Monash FODMAP ratings for psyllium, PHGG and oat fibre at bar-relevant doses. Check the Monash app or Monash testing directly.
