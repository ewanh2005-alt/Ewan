## Customer persona
A persona is your typical or most likely customer, **based on the evidence gathered so far**. It is not an individual person. It is a summary of the patterns and insights from your research. If you find yourself writing something no interviewee mentioned, ask whether it's evidence or assumption.

Build it from your interview notes:
- **Who are they?** The characteristics that appeared most often: age and stage of life; occupation or role; location; relevant interests or behaviours.
- **What are they trying to achieve?** The goals interviewees mentioned; what they're trying to improve, solve or achieve; what success looks like for them.
- **What challenges do they face?** Recurring frustrations, friction and pain points; problems that came up repeatedly; barriers stopping them reaching their goals; what they find difficult, time-consuming or expensive.
- **How do they solve the problem now?** The alternatives they use; competitors mentioned; workarounds they've created.
- **Where do they look for information, and who do they trust?** Social media platforms, online communities, professional networks, friends and colleagues, industry publications.
- **How does your solution help?** Which problem it solves; why that matters to them; what makes your approach different or better.
- **Give the persona a name**, and use it to test decisions ("Would Billy use this?").

## How to research a competitor
The goal is to understand their product, market position, progress and potential impact on your business, using multiple independent sources.
1. **Start with public information:** company websites, LinkedIn, press releases and news, patent and trademark databases, industry reports, conference talks and webinars, funding announcements and company filings. Build a profile covering product and key features, customer segments, pricing model, partnerships, technical approach, funding and growth, and stage of development.
2. **Talk to the ecosystem:** potential customers, industry experts, suppliers and partners, investors, trade associations, consultants and advisors. Ask: what problem is being solved? How credible is the solution? What barriers to adoption exist? How developed is the market? What alternatives are customers considering? A competitor making claims doesn't mean they deliver on them.
3. **Analyse the evidence:** what you know with confidence; what assumptions still need testing; what information is missing; where your own offering is differentiated. Don't treat marketing claims as facts until independently verified.
4. **Engage directly if necessary:** once you have clear questions, know what you're seeking, and the benefits outweigh the risks.
5. **Focus on what matters:** market readiness, customer adoption, pricing and business model, technical strengths and weaknesses, partnerships and distribution channels, competitive advantages and barriers to entry.
